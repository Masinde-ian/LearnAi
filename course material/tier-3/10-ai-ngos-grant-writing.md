# AI for NGOs & Community Organizations: Reporting & Grant Writing

**Overview**

Your organization does the real work: clean water, education, health outreach, women's empowerment. But donors never see the field work — they see proposals, reports, and numbers. This course shows you how to use AI as a drafting assistant for grant proposals, donor reports, M&E summaries, and stakeholder communication, without ever letting it invent your data. The one clear win: a proposal and reporting system that gets your good work written down, funded, and credited.

**Who this is for**

- Small NGO and CBO staff who write proposals and donor reports in their spare time
- Project officers who must produce M&E summaries on deadline
- Community groups seeking local and international funding
- Program staff who want to communicate results without losing honesty

**What you'll be able to do**

- Draft grant proposals and donor reports with AI
- Build M&E (monitoring & evaluation) summaries efficiently
- Create outreach and awareness content for your programs
- Manage stakeholder communication with AI-assisted drafting
- Stay accurate and honest — AI as a drafting tool, not a data source

## Module 1: The AI honesty rule that protects your whole org

Before any prompt, before any document, learn the one rule that keeps your organization safe: AI is a drafting tool, never a data source.

**What that means in practice:**

- AI can structure, rephrase, summarize, and draft. It cannot observe your beneficiaries, count your attendance, or read your records.
- Every number in a proposal or report must come from your own data — verified, dated, and traceable.
- AI has been known to invent plausible-sounding statistics, citations, and "facts." Never paste them into a donor document.
- Donors in Kenya and internationally audit claims. A single invented figure can end a grant and damage your reputation.

**The golden workflow:**

1. Collect your own data first (attendance lists, M&E records, receipts, photos)
2. Ask AI to draft the language around that data
3. Read every output, check every fact, fix every number
4. Save the verified version as your source of truth

**Do this:** tell every staff member the rule in writing: "AI drafts; we verify; numbers come from our records."

**Not this:** asking AI "how many girls completed primary school in Kenya" and pasting the answer into a proposal. Verify with a real source (KNBS, the ministry, your own data) or leave the number out.

**TEMPLATE: Fact-check checklist**

```
FACT-CHECK CHECKLIST - document: [name]
Every number has a source: [yes/no - list source]
Dates match our records: [yes/no]
Names of partners/donors spelled correctly: [yes/no]
No AI-invented statistics: [yes/no]
All claims match what actually happened: [yes/no]
Version saved as verified: [file name/date]
```

## Module 2: Draft grant proposals that get read

A donor reads dozens of proposals. Yours wins by being clear, specific, and tied to real evidence — not by being long or flowery.

**The structure donors expect (adapt per call):**

- **Executive summary** — the problem, your solution, the ask, in one page
- **Problem statement** — with local evidence and your community's reality
- **Goal and objectives** — one goal, 2-4 measurable objectives
- **Activities** — what you will actually do, when, with whom
- **M&E plan** — how you will prove results
- **Budget** — realistic, explained, aligned to activities
- **Organizational capacity** — who you are, what you have delivered

**How AI helps:**

- AI drafts each section from your notes and data
- AI tightens your language to the donor's word limits
- AI rephrases your draft to match a specific donor's tone and priorities
- AI never supplies your numbers — you do

**Do this:** read the call for proposals (CFP) three times before drafting. Answer exactly what it asks, in its order.

**Not this:** submitting the same proposal to five donors with only the names changed. Donors compare notes, and generic proposals read as lazy.

**TEMPLATE: Proposal outline**

```
PROPOSAL OUTLINE
Donor and call: [name and reference]
Deadline: [date]
Our ask: [KES/USD amount]
Problem (with 2-3 real data points from our records): [text]
Goal: [one sentence]
Objectives: [2-4 measurable ones]
Activities with timeline: [what, when, who]
M&E plan: [indicators, tools, frequency]
Budget summary: [major lines]
Our track record: [previous projects and results]
```

## Module 3: Donor reports that keep the money coming

Most grants are lost at the reporting stage, not the proposal stage. A donor who trusts your reports renews your funding. A donor who reads vagueness and missed deadlines hesitates.

**The reporting rhythm:**

- Understand exactly what the report must cover (some donors have templates and strict formats)
- Collect evidence as you go — do not try to remember a quarter of activities at the end
- Structure: summary, activities completed, results against indicators, challenges, finances, next steps
- Submit on time, every time

**How AI helps:**

- AI turns your raw activity notes into a clear, structured narrative
- AI drafts the "results against indicators" table text from your real numbers
- AI writes the challenges-and-lessons section honestly
- AI tightens your draft to the donor's format and length

**Do this:** keep a running activity log — a simple Google Sheet updated weekly is enough. A log is a draft report already written.

**Not this:** making progress sound bigger than it was. Report actual numbers, and if a target was missed, say why and what you will change.

**TEMPLATE: Donor report log**

```
REPORT LOG - project: [name]
Week | Activity | Participants | Outcome | Evidence (photos/docs) | Notes
[paste rows as you go]
```

## Module 4: Build M&E summaries efficiently

M&E is where organizations drown — raw attendance sheets, survey forms, and field notes that nobody turns into findings. AI turns your own collected data into summaries that management, boards, and donors can read.

**The M&E summary workflow:**

1. Keep indicators defined at project start: what you measure, how, and how often
2. Collect data in simple forms (Google Forms work well for mobile) or attendance sheets
3. Feed your real numbers into AI and ask for a structured summary
4. AI produces: results against targets, notable changes, outliers, and gaps
5. You verify every figure against the raw data before sharing

**What AI does well:**

- Formats raw tables into readable summaries
- Drafts indicator progress statements from your numbers
- Identifies patterns you describe ("attendance dropped in April — check the raw data")
- Builds simple dashboard descriptions for a board report

**What AI cannot do:**

- Interview your beneficiaries, count your inputs, or observe your program
- Fix missing data — gaps must be flagged honestly, not filled in

**Do this:** define your indicators before the program starts. M&E is harder to invent mid-project.

**Not this:** asking AI to "summarize the impact of our program" and letting it guess what happened. AI does not know what happened — your data does.

**TEMPLATE: Indicator tracking table**

```
INDICATOR TRACKING - project: [name]
Indicator | Baseline | Target | Current | Data source | Frequency | Verifier
[paste rows]
```

## Module 5: Outreach and awareness content for programs

Your programs depend on communities knowing they exist: health drives, enrollment campaigns, training announcements, awareness days. AI writes the content; you know your community.

**The outreach toolkit AI can draft:**

- WhatsApp and SMS messages in plain language
- Posters and flyers text (design them in Canva Magic Studio)
- Radio and community announcement scripts
- Social media posts for Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok
- School and church announcement blurbs
- Simple fact sheets on your program

**The community rules:**

- Translate and localize — English plus Kiswahili, Sheng, or local languages where your community lives
- Use plain language; avoid donor jargon
- Include the essential details: what, where, when, who, how to join
- Be careful with sensitive topics (health, gender, disability) — respectful wording matters

**Do this:** test every outreach message with a community member before publishing. If they do not understand it, rewrite it.

**Not this:** posting English-only, jargon-heavy announcements to a community that speaks Kiswahili and lives on WhatsApp.

**TEMPLATE: Outreach message brief**

```
OUTREACH BRIEF
Program/event: [name]
Audience: [who and where]
Goal: [inform / recruit / invite / change behavior]
Key details: [what, where, when, who, how to join]
Language: [English / Kiswahili / other]
Channel: [WhatsApp / SMS / poster / radio / social]
Sensitive points: [what to word carefully]
```

## Module 6: Stakeholder communication with AI-assisted drafting

You answer to many people: donors, county officials, community leaders, partners, your board, and the public. Each needs a different tone and format, and AI drafts them fast.

**The stakeholder toolkit:**

- **Donor updates** — short progress emails between formal reports
- **Community leaders** — respect-laden briefing notes
- **County and national government** — formal letters and memos
- **Partners** — collaborative updates and meeting notes
- **Board** — structured summaries with numbers and decisions needed
- **Public** — transparency posts and thank-you messages

**The drafting rule for all of it:**

- Give AI the facts, the tone, and the audience
- Draft once, edit twice, send only what you would sign your name to
- Never have AI address someone by name you have not personally approved
- Keep a record of what was sent and when

**Do this:** create a saved prompt for each stakeholder type so drafting takes five minutes, not an hour.

**Not this:** sending AI output word-for-word without checking the tone. A formal letter to a county office written in WhatsApp slang is a credibility disaster.

**TEMPLATE: Stakeholder message log**

```
STAKEHOLDER MESSAGE LOG
Date | Stakeholder | Purpose | Format | Key facts included | Sent by | Notes
[paste rows]
```

## Prompt Library

Copy, fill in the blanks, and adapt.

**Proposal executive summary**

Use to open a proposal cleanly.

```
Draft an executive summary for a grant proposal. Our organization [name] does [work] in [location]. The problem: [problem with our real data]. Our solution: [activities]. We are requesting [amount] for [duration]. Keep it under 250 words, clear and plain, suitable for a donor who reads fast. No invented statistics.
```

**Problem statement from local evidence**

Use to build the problem section.

```
Help me write the problem statement section of a proposal. The issue is [problem]. Our real evidence: [list actual observations, attendance, community reports, local data]. The affected community is [describe]. Frame it with dignity — the community is capable, not helpless. Keep it under 300 words with only the evidence I gave you.
```

**Goals and objectives**

Use to make your plan measurable.

```
Turn our program plan into measurable goals and objectives. Our program does [activities] for [community]. Turn this into: one goal, 3-4 SMART objectives (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound), and one indicator per objective. Base everything on our real program design. Format as a table.
```

**Activities and timeline**

Use to show donors the plan.

```
Write the activities and timeline section of a proposal. Our activities over [duration]: [list with rough months]. Format as a simple month-by-month or quarter-by-quarter table showing activity, who runs it, and expected participants. Keep it realistic for a small organization.
```

**Budget narrative**

Use to explain your budget lines.

```
Here is our proposed budget: [paste budget lines]. Write a short budget narrative explaining each major line (staff, activities, materials, M&E, transport) and why the amounts are realistic for [location]. Note where costs are shared or contributed in kind. Keep it under 300 words, transparent and honest.
```

**M&E plan section**

Use to show donors you will measure results.

```
Write the M&E plan section for our proposal. Our indicators: [paste indicator table]. Our data collection: [forms, attendance, interviews, frequency]. Who verifies: [roles]. Explain how results will be reported to the donor. Keep it under 250 words and make it match our real capacity.
```

**Donor report summary**

Use for the opening of a report.

```
Write the summary section of a donor report. Our project [name] in [location] ran [activities]. Against our targets [paste indicator table with actual vs target], here is what we achieved: [paste real numbers]. Challenges: [list honestly]. Keep it under 200 words, factual, no exaggeration.
```

**Results against indicators**

Use to present M&E numbers clearly.

```
Here is our indicator data for this reporting period: [paste table]. Format it as a clean results-against-targets section: indicator, baseline, target, actual, status (achieved/partial/not met). Then write 2-3 short paragraphs explaining the numbers, including one honest gap. Use only the data I gave you.
```

**Challenges and lessons learned**

Use to stay credible with donors.

```
Write the challenges and lessons learned section of our report. What happened: [describe honestly, including what went wrong]. What we changed: [real adjustments]. What we will do differently next period: [plan]. Frame setbacks as learning, not excuses. Keep it under 250 words.
```

**Financial report narrative**

Use to explain spend.

```
Here is our spending against the approved budget: [paste lines]. Write a short narrative for the donor report: overall spend vs budget, the 2 biggest lines and why, any variance and the reason, and confirmation that records are available for audit. Transparent, under 200 words.
```

**Indicator progress statement**

Use when only the data table exists.

```
Turn this raw data into a readable progress statement: [paste raw numbers]. Write: overall status, the strongest indicator, the weakest indicator, and what the data suggests for the next quarter. Flag any gaps in the data clearly rather than guessing. Under 200 words.
```

**Donor update email**

Use between formal reports.

```
Draft a short donor update email for [donor name] about [project]. Include: one line of thanks, 2-3 concrete updates with real numbers, one photo or milestone if available, and the next milestone and date. Keep it under 150 words, warm but professional.
```

**Outreach WhatsApp message**

Use to reach communities.

```
Write a WhatsApp outreach message for [program/event] in [location]. Audience: [describe community]. Key details: [what, where, when, who, how to join]. Language: [English/Kiswahili/other]. Plain words, respectful, under 80 words, with a clear way to respond.
```

**Poster and flyer text**

Use with Canva to make designs.

```
Write the text for a poster/flyer promoting [program/event]. Include: a headline under 8 words, 3 short bullet points, the key details line, and a contact or registration line. Plain language for [audience], [language]. Keep the whole flyer readable at arm's length.
```

**Radio and community announcement script**

Use for local stations and gatherings.

```
Write a 30-second community announcement script for [program/event]. It will be read aloud in [language]. Include: what it is, who it is for, when and where, how to join, and a warm closing. Speak in short sentences that are easy to read on air.
```

**Letter to county official**

Use for formal government communication.

```
Draft a formal letter to [official and office] about [topic, e.g. our program in their area / a request for support]. Include: introduction of our organization, the purpose of the letter, specific request, our contact details, and a respectful closing. Formal English, under 300 words, ready for our letterhead.
```

**Board summary**

Use for board meetings.

```
Write a board meeting summary for our organization. Key updates: [paste bullet points of real facts and numbers]. Decisions needed from the board: [list]. Risks: [list honestly]. Keep it structured, under 250 words, with numbers for anything measurable.
```

**Stakeholder thank-you message**

Use to maintain relationships.

```
Write a thank-you message to [stakeholder, e.g. community leaders / partner / volunteer group] after [event or milestone]. Mention specifically what they contributed and the real outcome. Keep it warm, genuine, and under 100 words, suitable for WhatsApp or email.
```

**Honesty audit prompt**

Use before sending anything important.

```
Act as an editor. Here is a draft document: [paste]. Check it for: any numbers or claims without a clear source, any language that overstates results, any missing dates or names, and anything that could mislead a donor or the public. List exactly what to fix and where. Do not change the facts — only flag them.
```

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Letting AI invent statistics, citations, or outcomes
- Pasting AI output into donor documents unread
- Reporting success instead of actual progress
- Keeping M&E notes in memory instead of a log
- Writing proposals in donor jargon communities and staff do not use
- Submitting one generic proposal to many donors
- Ignoring report deadlines — funding is lost on lateness
