# AI Music Production Basics: Beats, Lyrics & Distribution

**Overview** — You learned to write lyrics and make a rough demo. This course takes you further: generating and refining beats with AI, arranging a full song, recording and mixing at home, and getting your music onto streaming platforms where Kenyans actually listen. The one clear win: you finish and distribute one full, release-ready song — and learn how the money flows.

**Who this is for**

- Songwriters who have demos but no finished, released songs
- Gospel, gengetone, and afro-pop artists recording from home
- Producers who want AI-assisted beats and a beat-selling side business
- Anyone curious about streaming royalties and Kenyan platforms
- Creators who want to sell instrumentals to other artists for cash

**What you'll be able to do**

- Generate and refine beats and instrumentals with AI tools
- Structure a full song from intro to outro and write every section
- Record and mix vocals at home on a phone or basic setup
- Distribute to Spotify, Audiomack, Boomplay, and Mdundo
- Understand royalties, streams, and how to sell beats via M-Pesa

## Lesson 1: From demo to a repeatable production system

A rough demo is an idea; a released song is a product. Between them sits a system you repeat every time:

1. Write and finalise lyrics with AI help
2. Generate or pick the beat and refine it
3. Arrange the song: intro, verse, chorus, bridge, outro
4. Record your vocals at home
5. Mix so it sounds clean on a phone speaker
6. Prepare release details (title, art, metadata)
7. Distribute to streaming platforms
8. Track and understand your royalties

Do not skip steps. Artists who release fast but skip mixing and metadata get fewer streams. The system is your quality gate.

## Lesson 2: Beats and instrumentals with AI

You have two paths to a beat:

- **AI generation**: Suno and Udio can create full instrumentals from a text prompt. Describe the mood, tempo, and style: "high-energy gengetone, 104 BPM, strong kick drum, log drum." Generate 4-6 takes and pick the strongest. You can also generate instrumental-only tracks to avoid vocal clash.
- **Beat sites**: buy or download instrumentals from sites that license Kenyan-friendly genres. Check the license — some are free for non-commercial use only; "exclusive rights" means no one else can use it.

Refining a beat means adjusting energy: a beat that drags in the verse needs the percussion simplified; a chorus needs the drum and bass to hit harder. Ask AI for arrangement advice, not magic. Your ear still decides.

## Lesson 3: Structuring a full song

Kenyans listen to songs on repeat — the structure decides whether they replay it. The standard shape:

- **Intro** (4-8 bars): the hook-instrumental or a vocal tease
- **Verse 1**: set the story, low energy
- **Chorus**: the hook, big energy, repeatable
- **Verse 2**: develop the story
- **Bridge**: a change in mood or key before the final chorus
- **Outro**: a calm landing

For gengetone, keep verses short and the chorus sticky. For gospel, let the chorus build emotionally. Write each section against the beat, not before it. A song that feels too long for one genre will feel too short for another — match the section lengths to the genre and the beat.

## Lesson 4: Home recording basics

You do not need a studio. A decent phone mic, a quiet room, and a cloth (to reduce echo) can produce an acceptable vocal track.

- Record in a small room with curtains or clothes to absorb echo
- Put the phone or mic 15-25 cm from your mouth, slightly off-axis
- Record dry (no effects), the mixing stage adds them later
- Record 2-3 takes of each section and pick the best
- Use free editing apps (CapCut, Audacity, or phone DAWs) to cut mistakes

Record the same volume throughout — loud passages and soft passages at similar levels make mixing much easier. If you buy a USB mic later, the same rules apply.

## Lesson 5: Mixing basics at home

Mixing is where a demo becomes a track. Keep it simple:

- **Levels**: balance the vocal over the beat so every word is clear
- **EQ**: cut low rumble from the vocal (low-pass), give the beat space for the vocal
- **Compression**: evens out loud and quiet vocal parts — use it lightly
- **Reverb**: a touch adds space; too much makes the vocal muddy
- **Loudness**: make the final track consistently loud, but do not over-boost

Check your mix on three places: phone speaker, headphones, and in the car or a matatu. If the vocal is clear and the chorus hits harder than the verse, you are 90 percent there. Ask AI for "what to fix" advice by describing what you hear, not for a magic button.

## Lesson 6: Getting your music on streaming platforms

Distribution gets your song onto Spotify, Apple Music, Boomplay, Audiomack, and Mdundo. Distributors like DistroKid and TuneCore charge a fee (often a yearly subscription) and deliver your track everywhere in one upload.

Kenya-specific reality:

- **Mdundo** is dominant in Kenya and free to upload for artists (it makes money from ads)
- **Boomplay** is strong across Africa and important for a Kenyan audience
- **Audiomack** is popular with African audiences and free for artists
- **Spotify and Apple Music** pay more per stream but are a smaller share of Kenyan listening

Prepare before uploading: final audio file, square cover art (1200x1200 minimum), title, artist name, and metadata (genre, release date). Pick your artist name once and use it everywhere — consistency builds a brand.

## Lesson 7: Understanding royalties

Every stream generates a small payment — in the range of roughly $0.003-0.005 per stream on major platforms, paid out months later through your distributor. That means:

- 1,000 streams earns a tiny amount; do not expect a hit from streams alone
- Royalties from ad-based platforms like Mdundo are lower per stream but add up
- The money grows with a catalogue: 50 songs earning a little each month beats one song earning nothing
- Performance royalties (radio, TV, live venues) go through a collection body — register if you plan to chase them

Do not quit your job for a single release. Treat streaming as the storefront that builds your name; the real income for most Kenyan artists comes from shows, brand deals, and selling beats.

## Lesson 8: Selling beats and the M-Pesa market

Producers earn directly by selling beats to artists, and you can do this with the same AI tools:

- Set prices: lease (someone else uses the beat) from KES 2,000-5,000; exclusive rights from KES 8,000-25,000
- Post 15-30 second beat previews on TikTok, Instagram, and WhatsApp status
- Take payment via M-Pesa till or paybill, then send the full beat and a simple licence note
- Keep a beat catalogue: name, BPM, key, genre, price, and who licensed it

Selling beats is the fastest cash in music — the buyer pays now and handles their own distribution. Pair a few beat sales per month with your streaming catalogue and you have a real music side business.

## Prompt Library

**Production workflow**

**How to use it:** Run every project through this system.

```
Create a step-by-step music production workflow for an AI-assisted artist working from home in Kenya. Steps: lyrics, beat, arrangement, recording, mixing, release prep, distribution, royalties tracking. For each step, give a time estimate and the exact tool to use. Keep it to 8 steps I can follow on a phone.
```

**Beat style prompt**

**How to use it:** Feed this into Suno or Udio to generate instrumentals.

```
Write a one-line style prompt to generate a [genre] beat with this feel: [describe mood, e.g. dark, energetic, romantic]. Include tempo (BPM), the main instruments, and the energy of each song section. Keep it under 25 words so it fits AI music tools.
```

**Beat selection advice**

**How to use it:** When you have several takes and cannot choose.

```
I generated 4 AI beats for a [genre] song about [topic]. Help me choose: what should I listen for in the first 10 seconds, in the chorus, and in the drop? Give me a short checklist to rate each beat out of 10, then tell me how to pick.
```

**Full song arrangement**

**How to use it:** Lay out the whole song before writing.

```
Plan the arrangement for a [genre] song about [topic], length about [3-4] minutes. Use this shape: intro, verse 1, chorus, verse 2, bridge, chorus, outro. For each section give the number of bars, the energy level, and what the section must accomplish. Make the chorus the section with the most energy.
```

**Section-by-section lyrics**

**How to use it:** Write one section at a time against the beat.

```
Write lyrics for the [VERSE 2] of my [genre] song about [topic]. Match the mood of verse 1, move the story forward, and end with a line that leads into the chorus. Keep the same rhyme style as: [PASTE VERSE 1]. Mix in a little Kiswahili-English where it fits naturally.
```

**Chorus hook variations**

**How to use it:** Test different hooks before you commit.

```
Give me 4 different chorus (hook) options for a [genre] song about [topic]. Vary them: one short and repeated, one with a question, one mixing Kiswahili and English, one emotional. Each should be under 2 lines of 12 bars and easy to remember. I will pick the strongest.
```

**Intro and outro ideas**

**How to use it:** Strong openers and clean endings.

```
Give me 3 intro ideas and 3 outro ideas for a [genre] song about [topic]. Intros should hook a listener in the first 5 seconds (a vocal line, a sound effect, or an instrumental tease). Outros should land the song calmly. Match the energy of the rest of the song.
```

**Recording checklist**

**How to use it:** Set up before you press record.

```
Create a home recording checklist for a phone setup in a small room: room choice, reducing echo with cloth, mic distance, consistent volume, 2-3 takes per section, and what to check before recording each take. Keep it to 8 items I can tick off my phone.
```

**Mixing troubleshooting**

**How to use it:** Describe what you hear, get targeted fixes.


## Common mistakes to avoid

- Releasing a demo as a final track without mixing
- Ignoring metadata and cover art — they decide whether you get playlisted
- Putting a song only on Spotify when your audience is on Mdundo and Boomplay
- Expecting streams alone to pay the rent
- Selling beats without a licence note or payment record
- Using a different artist name on every platform
- Comparing your first release to finished studio tracks
