# AI Content Writing Business: Get Paid to Write with AI

## Overview

The Tier 1 course taught you to write better posts with AI. This course turns that skill into income: writing articles, product descriptions, and website copy that clients pay for; editing AI output until it reads like high-quality human writing; finding gigs locally and on freelance platforms; and pricing and managing multiple clients. You will finish with a repeatable process — brief to delivered work — that produces paid projects instead of one-off tasks.

## Who this is for

- Bloggers and writers who want to get paid for the skill they already have
- Freelancers who want a steady writing service line alongside other work
- Anyone who has used AI to write but does not know how to sell it
- People who want remote or local writing income without showing a face

## Lesson 1: Know what clients pay for

Clients do not pay for "AI writing." They pay for outcomes: a blog post that ranks, a product description that sells, a website that makes them look professional. Learn the four main product types.

**Articles and blog posts.** The most common gig. For news sites, company blogs, and media outlets.

**Product descriptions.** E-commerce sellers need short, selling copy for every product on their shop.

**Website copy.** Homepages, About pages, and service pages. Higher value, fewer clients, better pay.

**Social content.** Captions and short posts, often packaged monthly.

**Do this:** learn one format deeply first — articles. Mastery of one format wins repeat clients faster than a little of everything.

**Not this:** accepting every job type you cannot do well. One badly delivered job hurts your reputation more than five missed opportunities.

## Lesson 2: The brief-to-delivery process

Professional writers follow a process. It protects quality and makes every job repeatable:

1. Clarify the brief: topic, audience, length, tone, deadline.
2. Research: gather facts, examples, and any client notes.
3. Outline: structure the piece before writing a word.
4. Draft: write with AI from your outline, not from scratch in one go.
5. Edit: the human pass that makes it yours.
6. Deliver: clean file, on time, with a short note.

**Do this:** send the outline to the client for approval before drafting. It prevents expensive rewrites later.

**Not this:** writing a full draft from a two-line brief. Half of all rework comes from unclear instructions.

## Lesson 3: The human pass — editing AI output to high quality

AI drafts fast, but it also writes flat. The difference between a beginner and a paid professional is the edit. Run every draft through the human pass:

- Rewrite the intro in your own words — first impressions matter most
- Cut AI tells: "in today's fast-paced world," "furthermore," "in conclusion"
- Break up long, perfectly-rhythmed sentences
- Add real specifics: prices, places, examples, opinions
- Read it aloud and fix anything that stumbles

**Do this:** aim for the standard that a client cannot tell AI was involved. The goal is not to hide the tool, it is to deliver work that reads human.

**Not this:** submitting AI's first draft "because the client will never know." Clients who read well can tell, and your reputation is the product.

## Lesson 4: Build a portfolio that wins gigs

Nobody pays a stranger with no samples. A small portfolio solves that. You do not need real clients to start — spec work counts.

Write 3 sample pieces:

- One article on a topic you know well
- One product description set (e.g. 5 items from a Kenyan shop)
- One homepage/About page for a hypothetical small business

Publish them on a free Medium page or a Google Drive folder you can share. A simple one-page "portfolio" document with links is enough to start.

**Do this:** write samples in niches you actually know. A finance student writing finance samples wins finance gigs.

**Not this:** waiting until you have "real" clients to build a portfolio. You need samples first to get the first client.

## Lesson 5: Find gigs — freelance platforms and local work

Two channels: global and local. Use both.

**Global platforms:** Upwork and Fiverr pay in dollars but are competitive. Start with small jobs, a complete profile, and a strong proposal. Create a Fiverr gig or apply to Upwork jobs in writing niches.

**Local Kenyan work:** NGOs, media outlets, and small companies constantly need content. Pitch them directly by email or WhatsApp with a short, specific offer.

**Do this:** send 3 pitches a week, every week, without fail. Freelancing is a numbers game — your conversion rate times your pitch volume equals your income.

**Not this:** waiting for jobs to find you. The writers who work are the ones who pitch.

## Lesson 6: Price your services

Pricing feels awkward until you have a system. Two common models:

**Per word:** typical international entry rates run $0.01-0.05 per word. A 1,000-word article at $0.02 earns $20 (about KES 2,600). Rates rise with experience and niche.

**Per article or package:** many Kenyan clients prefer a flat fee. Local rates vary widely — from KES 500 for a short blog post to KES 5,000-15,000 for longer, researched pieces or website copy.

**Do this:** start at a price that gets you work, then raise it every 3-4 completed projects. Track your rate per hour — if a job pays under your target hourly rate, it is too cheap.

**Not this:** competing purely on price forever. Undercutting every Kenyan freelancer makes the market worse for everyone, including you.

## Lesson 7: Manage multiple clients like a professional

Multiple clients fail when you have no system. The professional method:

- One master list: client, project, deadline, price, payment status
- Templates for proposals, invoices, and follow-ups
- A content calendar per ongoing client
- A 24-hour rule: reply to every client within a day
- Payments before or on delivery, never months later

**Do this:** set a weekly delivery day for each client and protect it. Predictability keeps clients happy and you sane.

**Not this:** overbooking. One missed deadline does more damage than turning down work.

## Prompt Library

Copy, fill in the brackets, and adapt.

**Client brief questionnaire**

Use at the start of every job.

```
I am starting a writing job for a client. Write 8 questions to
ask them so I can deliver without back-and-forth: topic,
audience, length, tone, format, examples they like, deadline,
and what "done" means.
```

**Brief to outline**

Use to plan before drafting.

```
Turn this brief into a clear outline for [article type]: a
working title, 5 sections with a one-line point each, and an
angle for the intro: [paste brief]
```

**Article draft**

Use after the outline is approved.

```
Write a first draft from this outline in short paragraphs,
warm and direct, not academic. Keep facts accurate and do not
add claims beyond the brief. Add a strong hook and a clear
ending: [paste outline]
```

**Product description set**

Use for e-commerce clients.

```
Write product descriptions for these items: [list products].
For each: a 2-line hook, 3 bullet benefits, materials/features,
and a short "why buy" line. Selling tone, no emojis: [paste
details]
```

**Website homepage copy**

Use for small business websites.

```
Write homepage copy for a [business type] in [town]. Include a
headline, a subheadline, 3 value points, a short about
paragraph, and a call to action. Under 300 words, warm and
confident.
```

**About and service page**

Use for the pages clients forget.

```
Write an About page and a service page for a [business type].
Focus on the customer's problem and the result the business
delivers. Under 250 words each, in a trustworthy tone.
```

**De-AI edit pass**

Use on every draft before delivery.

```
Edit this draft until it reads like a human wrote it: rewrite
the intro, remove AI cliches like "in today's fast-paced
world," vary sentence length, and add a concrete detail where
possible. Keep all facts: [paste draft]
```

**Match a client's voice**

Use when the client has samples.

```
Here is how my client writes. Match this voice and style in the
following draft. Keep all facts and structure: [paste samples]
then [paste draft]
```

**Spec portfolio brief**

Use to build your 3 starter samples.

```
I want to build a writing portfolio. Suggest 3 spec samples for
a writer targeting [niche, e.g. Kenyan small business finance]:
one article, one product description set, and one website page.
Give me a topic and brief for each.
```

**Upwork proposal**

Use to win a freelance job.

```
Write a short proposal for this Upwork job: [paste job text].
Mention my relevant experience, one specific idea for the
client's content, and a reasonable price. Under 150 words,
professional, not generic.
```

**Local pitch email**

Use to pitch Kenyan businesses directly.

```
Write a short email pitching my writing service to a [business
type] in [town]. Mention I can write [articles/product
descriptions/website copy] and give a sample starting price.
Include a small, concrete idea for their business. Under 120
words.
```

**Quote builder**

Use to price a job fairly.

```
Help me price this writing job: [describe job, word count,
research needed, deadline]. Suggest a per-word rate, a flat
fee in KES, and a US-dollar equivalent for international
clients. Explain the reasoning briefly.
```

**Client onboarding message**

Use to start every engagement clearly.

```
Write a friendly onboarding message for a new writing client.
Confirm the project, the price, the deadline, how many
revisions are included, and how they can reach me. Keep it
professional and short.
```

**Client content calendar**

Use for ongoing monthly clients.

```
Build a monthly content calendar for a client in [industry].
Suggest [X] pieces of content: topics, formats, and a posting
schedule. Keep topics practical for a Kenyan audience.
```

**Progress report to client**

Use between delivery and payment.

```
Write a short progress/status message for my client. Confirm
what was delivered, what is next, and when payment of KES
[amount] is expected. Friendly, clear, professional.
```

## Action plan

1. Pick your strongest format (articles, product descriptions, or website copy).
2. Build 3 spec samples and publish them where clients can see them.
3. Set your starter price using the quote builder.
4. Send 3 pitches this week — one platform, two local.
5. Land your first paid job and run the full brief-to-delivery process.
6. Deliver with a short note and ask for a testimonial.
7. Add the job to your client list and start the next pitch.
