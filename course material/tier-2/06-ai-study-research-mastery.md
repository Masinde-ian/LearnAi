# AI Study & Research Mastery for Students

## Overview

The Tier 1 course showed you how AI explains topics and revises notes. This course builds the complete system around it: researching papers with proper citations, summarizing and comparing literature, building a study plan from your own syllabus, and preparing for exams across multiple subjects — all inside academic integrity rules. By the end, AI works for you as a research assistant and revision coach, not a shortcut around your own thinking.

## Who this is for

- University and college students writing term papers and research proposals
- Secondary students who want a repeatable revision system for KCSE
- Students who can use AI but are unsure how to cite sources properly
- Anyone worried about using AI and getting caught or going wrong

## What you'll be able to do

- Turn a vague topic into a sharp, answerable research question
- Summarize and compare multiple sources into a mini literature review
- Cite sources correctly in APA and other common styles
- Build a personalized study plan straight from your syllabus and exam dates
- Prepare for exams across several subjects at once with a rotation system

## Lesson 1: From vague topic to research question

Great research starts with a question, not a topic. "Climate change" is a topic — too big to research. "How does climate change affect maize yields in central Kenya?" is a question you can actually answer with evidence.

Use AI to sharpen your topic before you touch any sources.

**Do this:** run the "sharpen your question" prompt, pick the strongest question, and check it is answerable — you should be able to name the evidence that would answer it.

**Not this:** researching "climate change in Africa" for a 10-page paper. A vague question produces a vague, unfocused paper.

Once you have a sharp question, let AI turn it into search keywords. Use those keywords in Google Scholar, Perplexity, or NotebookLM. The rule: AI plans the search, you pick the sources. AI should never list sources it cannot show you.

## Lesson 2: Read and summarize literature in half the time

Journal articles are dense. Read them smarter: upload each paper into NotebookLM and ask questions about it — what the study did, how, and what it found. The answer comes back with citations, so you can check it against the actual page.

**Do this:** keep one short summary per source: question, method, finding, and your one-line comment. Five short summaries are easier to revise than five full papers.

**Not this:** pasting a whole paper and asking "summarise this" in one go. Ask specific questions, source by source, so you actually understand each one.

Build your summaries over a week, one paper at a time. When the essay deadline comes, you are not re-reading papers — you are re-reading your own notes. That is the entire trick.

## Lesson 3: Build a literature review and comparison matrix

A literature review is not a list of summaries. It is a conversation between sources: who agrees, who disagrees, and where the gap is. After summarizing 5-10 sources, get AI to build a comparison matrix.

The matrix rows are your sources. The columns are author/year, question, method, finding, and gap. When you look across the columns, patterns appear — most studies use small samples, everyone measures yields differently, nobody covers your county. That pattern is the argument of your review.

**Do this:** write the "gap" column honestly. If a source does not cover your specific question, say so. The gap is where your paper makes its contribution.

**Not this:** producing a matrix where every row says "very interesting." If all sources agree, note it — then look for the disagreements, because those make a stronger paper.

## Lesson 4: Citations and referencing done right

Getting citations wrong loses marks and can trigger an academic dishonesty case. Learn the basics of APA and let AI do the formatting — but verify everything.

APA in-text citation: (Mutua, 2024). The reference list entry includes the author, year, title, and source details. The pattern is consistent, but small details (italics, punctuation, DOI) vary by source type.

**The golden rule:** AI can format a citation from details you give it, but it must never invent a source. If AI cannot show you where an idea came from, treat it as a hallucination and drop it.

**Do this:** check every reference against the real document before submitting. Paste the actual author names and year from the paper — never ask AI to "find the author" for you.

**Not this:** copying a citation AI invented and hoping nobody checks. Lecturers check. A fake reference is worse than no reference.

## Lesson 5: Build your personalized study plan

The Tier 1 course helped you study smarter one session at a time. Now build the schedule. Feed AI your syllabus topics, exam dates, and the hours you can realistically study.

A good plan spaces topics out so you revise each one more than once before the exam: first pass to learn, second pass to recall, third pass to test. That repetition is what moves a topic from short-term memory to exam-day recall.

**Do this:** be honest about your real hours. A plan that assumes 6 hours a day when you can give 2 will fail by week one.

**Not this:** planning a perfect schedule you never follow. A realistic schedule you follow for three weeks beats an ideal one you abandon.

## Lesson 6: Multi-subject exam prep system

Preparing for five subjects at once fails when you cram one and forget the others. The fix is a rotation: cycle through subjects on a schedule, generate practice questions per subject, and mark yourself closed-book.

For each subject, keep a running list of your weak spots — the topics you get wrong in practice. Target those first in the next rotation, not the subjects you already enjoy.

**Do this:** mark your practice papers strictly. Generating questions is easy; the learning happens when you mark yourself honestly and go back to the weak topics.

**Not this:** jumping to the next subject the moment practice gets hard. The hard parts are exactly the ones you must repeat.

## Lesson 7: Academic integrity — the rules that keep you safe

This is the lesson that protects everything else. The line from Tier 1 still holds: AI is a tutor, not a ghostwriter. But the rules go further.

First, check your university or school policy. Many Kenyan institutions now have specific rules on AI use — some allow it for research and planning, some require you to declare any AI assistance. Know your policy before exam season, not after a suspicion.

Second, the safe patterns: AI explains concepts, helps you plan, checks your grammar, and generates practice questions. You do the reading, the writing, and the citing. If you paste AI's essay text and submit it as yours, that is plagiarism — detectable and heavily punished.

Third, where your institution allows it, disclose AI use honestly and cite AI tools like any other source. Being transparent is the difference between a student who uses AI responsibly and one who looks like they are hiding it.

**Do this:** before submitting, run the integrity self-check prompt on your work.

**Not this:** deciding the rules "don't apply to you" because everyone else uses AI. Detectors and policies exist, and a disciplinary case costs more than any mark.

## Prompt Library

Copy, fill in the brackets, and adapt.

**Sharpen your research question**

Use before any research to turn a topic into an answerable question.

```
I want to research [topic, e.g. climate change and farming in
Kenya]. Turn it into 5 sharp research questions a student could
answer in a term paper. Make them specific to Kenya, not general.
```

**Topic to search keywords**

Use to build a search plan for Google Scholar or Perplexity.

```
Give me 10 search keywords and phrases for this research
question: [question]. Mix English terms, and suggest where in
Kenya I might find local studies and reports.
```

**Paper summary**

Use for every source you read. Paste one paper at a time.

```
Summarise this paper for a student: the research question, the
method, the main findings, and one limitation. Quote key
sentences exactly so I can cite them. Then list 3 things I
should remember: [paste paper text]
```

**Comparison matrix**

Use after summarizing 5-10 sources.

```
Here are my summaries of [N] sources. Build a comparison matrix
with columns: author/year, research question, method, main
finding, and gap. Then tell me what patterns stand out across
all the sources: [paste summaries]
```

**APA citation**

Use when you have the source details in hand.

```
Format this as an APA in-text citation and a reference list
entry. Use only the details I give you — do not invent anything:
[author, year, title, journal or book, volume, pages, DOI or
publisher]
```

**Reference list builder**

Use to organize your references in order.

```
Here are my sources with details. Format all of them as an APA
reference list, alphabetised, and flag any details that look
incomplete or wrong: [paste details]
```

**Citation format checker**

Use before submitting to catch style mistakes.

```
Check these citations against APA rules and fix the formatting:
[paste your citations]. Tell me what you changed so I can learn.
```

**Personalized study plan**

Use once per subject or term. Paste your syllabus topics.

```
Build me a study plan for [subject] with exam on [date]. Here
are the topics: [list]. I can study [hours] per week. Plan each
topic to be revised 3 times before the exam, hardest topics
first. Keep it realistic for a Kenyan student's calendar.
```

**Weekly timetable**

Use to turn a plan into a schedule.

```
Turn this study plan into a weekly timetable that fits around my
classes and [other commitment]: [paste plan]. Keep study blocks
under 90 minutes with breaks. No emojis.
```

**Multi-subject rotation**

Use when revising several subjects.

```
I am preparing for these subjects: [list] with exams on [dates].
Make me a weekly rotation so each subject is practised once and
revised at least twice before its exam. Show it as a simple table.
```

**Practice paper generator**

Use for exam practice per subject.

```
Generate a practice paper for [subject] on [topics] in the
[KCSE/university] format: [X] short-answer and [Y] essay
questions. Give model answers separately so I can mark myself.
```

**Mark and improve my answer**

Use after attempting a practice question.

```
Here is my answer to [question]. Mark it out of 20, show where
I lost marks, and list the 3 most important things to improve.
Do not rewrite my answer for me: [paste answer]
```

**Paraphrase with citation practice**

Use to learn how to use a source in your own words.

```
Here is a passage from a source. Show me how to paraphrase it
in my own words AND cite it in APA. Then explain the difference
between this and plagiarism: [paste passage]
```

**Integrity self-check**

Use before submitting any assignment that used AI.

```
Here is my assignment and the notes I used. Check whether
anything reads like copied AI text or uncredited source
material. Flag any sentence that needs a citation or rewriting:
[paste work]
```

**Ask AI to teach, not answer**

Use when you feel tempted to copy an answer.

```
Do not give me the answer. Teach me how to solve [question] by
asking me guiding questions, one at a time, like a tutor.
```

6. Run your multi-subject rotation with practice papers.
7. Before submitting anything, run the integrity self-check.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Letting AI invent sources, citations, or page numbers
- Submitting AI-written text as your own work
- Researching a topic that is too broad to answer
- Summarizing 20 papers and never comparing them
- Building a study plan too ambitious to follow
- Not checking your institution's AI policy before submitting
